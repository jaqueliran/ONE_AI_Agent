

# Common Ground Teacher Handbook

**Version:** 1.0
**Audience:** Teachers and Academic Staff
**Document type:** Internal Pedagogical and Operational Guide

---

## 1. Purpose of This Handbook

This handbook provides teachers with a practical introduction to the pedagogical principles, teaching practices, and internal procedures of Common Ground.

It is intended to support new and current teachers in planning lessons, interacting with students, providing feedback, adapting instruction, and communicating with the Academic Coordination team.

This handbook should be read together with the **Academic Policy**, **Assessment Policy**, **Student Progression Policy**, and **Attendance & Make-up Policy**.

The handbook describes general teaching principles and procedures. When a specific institutional policy establishes a different rule, the applicable policy takes precedence.

---

# Part I. Pedagogical Principles

## 2. Communicative Approach

Common Ground follows a communicative approach to language teaching.

The primary purpose of language learning is to enable students to use English meaningfully in real or realistic contexts. Classroom activities should therefore provide opportunities for students to understand, produce, negotiate, and respond to meaning.

Teachers should prioritize:

* meaningful communication;
* interaction between students;
* contextualized language use;
* purposeful tasks;
* opportunities for spontaneous language production;
* development of communicative strategies;
* reflection on language use.

Grammar and vocabulary should be taught as resources that support communication rather than as isolated content whenever possible.

### 2.1 Classroom Interaction

Teachers should aim to maximize meaningful student participation.

Depending on the lesson objective, appropriate interaction patterns may include:

* individual work;
* pair work;
* small-group work;
* whole-group discussion;
* student-to-student interaction;
* teacher-student interaction.

Teacher talking time should not unnecessarily replace opportunities for students to communicate.

---

## 3. Learner Autonomy

Common Ground considers learners active participants in their own learning process.

Teachers should help students gradually develop the ability to:

* identify their learning needs;
* recognize their strengths and difficulties;
* set realistic learning goals;
* select appropriate learning strategies;
* monitor their own progress;
* evaluate their performance;
* seek and use feedback;
* continue learning outside the classroom.

Teachers should avoid creating unnecessary dependence on constant teacher correction or approval.

### 3.1 Supporting Autonomy

Teachers can promote autonomy by:

* explaining the purpose of activities;
* encouraging students to reflect on their performance;
* offering choices when appropriate;
* teaching learning strategies;
* encouraging students to use available resources independently;
* incorporating self-assessment;
* helping students formulate actionable goals.

---

## 4. Linguistic Diversity

Common Ground recognizes English as a diverse and global language.

Students may bring different linguistic backgrounds, accents, varieties of English, cultural references, and communication strategies to the classroom.

Teachers should:

* respect legitimate variation in English use;
* avoid treating one accent as inherently superior;
* distinguish communication problems from differences in linguistic variety;
* encourage students to adapt their communication to different contexts;
* discuss variation when it is relevant to learning objectives.

Language correction should focus on the student's communicative needs and the objectives of the activity.

---

## 5. English as a Lingua Franca

Common Ground adopts an English as a Lingua Franca perspective.

English is understood as a means of communication among people with different linguistic and cultural backgrounds, not exclusively as imitation of a native-speaker model.

Teaching should therefore emphasize:

* intelligibility;
* interaction;
* communicative effectiveness;
* negotiation of meaning;
* flexibility;
* awareness of different varieties of English.

A student's performance should not be considered deficient simply because it does not reproduce a particular native-speaker accent or variety.

---

## 6. Teacher Role

The teacher is responsible for designing learning experiences, facilitating interaction, observing student performance, providing feedback, and supporting learner development.

Teachers are expected to:

* plan lessons according to learning objectives;
* create opportunities for meaningful interaction;
* monitor student participation;
* provide appropriate feedback;
* adapt activities when necessary;
* document relevant student progress;
* communicate academic concerns to the appropriate team;
* maintain a respectful and inclusive learning environment.

The teacher is not expected to solve every student difficulty independently.

When a situation requires an institutional decision or intervention, the teacher should follow the appropriate escalation procedure.

---

## 7. Learner Role

Students are expected to participate actively in the learning process.

Students should be encouraged to:

* participate in classroom activities;
* communicate with classmates;
* complete assigned learning activities;
* reflect on their own progress;
* use feedback;
* communicate difficulties when they arise;
* take increasing responsibility for their learning.

Teachers should support students in developing these behaviors rather than assuming that all learners already possess them.

# Part II. Teaching Practices

## 8. Lesson Planning

Each lesson should have clear and observable learning objectives.

Before planning an activity, teachers should consider:

1. What should students be able to do by the end of the lesson?
2. What language or communicative resources support that objective?
3. What type of interaction will students need?
4. How will students practice the target skill?
5. How will the teacher know whether the objective was achieved?

A typical lesson may include:

* activation of previous knowledge;
* introduction or observation of new material;
* guided practice;
* meaningful communicative practice;
* reflection or feedback.

The sequence may vary according to the lesson objective and student needs.

---

## 9. Communicative Activities

Teachers should use activities that create genuine reasons for students to communicate.

Appropriate activities include:

### 9.1 Pair Work

Students work together to exchange information, solve problems, practice language, or discuss a topic.

### 9.2 Group Work

Small groups collaborate on discussions, projects, problem-solving activities, or decision-making tasks.

### 9.3 Discussions

Students express opinions, compare perspectives, ask questions, and respond to others.

### 9.4 Role-play

Students use English in simulated situations relevant to academic, professional, social, or everyday contexts.

### 9.5 Problem-solving

Students use English to analyze information, make decisions, negotiate solutions, or complete a task.

### 9.6 Projects

Projects may require students to combine language skills, research, collaboration, and communication to produce a meaningful outcome.

Activities should have a clear purpose rather than requiring interaction simply for the sake of interaction.

---

## 10. Feedback

Feedback should help students understand:

* what they are doing effectively;
* what needs improvement;
* why improvement is necessary;
* what they can do next.

Effective feedback should be:

* specific;
* relevant to the learning objective;
* understandable;
* actionable;
* respectful.

Whenever possible, feedback should focus on behaviors or performance rather than personal characteristics.

### 10.1 Feedback During Activities

Teachers do not need to interrupt every communicative activity to correct students.

During fluency-oriented activities, teachers may observe and record relevant language issues and address them afterward.

During accuracy-oriented activities, more immediate correction may be appropriate.

The type and timing of feedback should depend on the objective of the activity.

---

## 11. Error Correction

Error correction should be purposeful.

Teachers should consider:

* the objective of the activity;
* whether the error interferes with communication;
* whether the language point is currently being practiced;
* the student's level;
* whether correction would interrupt meaningful communication.

Possible strategies include:

* clarification requests;
* recasts;
* elicitation;
* prompts;
* peer correction;
* self-correction;
* explicit correction.

### 11.1 Fluency vs. Accuracy

During fluency activities, teachers should generally avoid excessive interruption.

During accuracy-focused activities, more explicit correction may be appropriate.

The goal is not to eliminate every error immediately. The goal is to support students in developing effective and increasingly controlled language use.

---

## 12. Differentiation

Students may have different levels of proficiency, learning histories, strengths, and areas for development.

Teachers should adapt activities when necessary.

Possible adaptations include:

* varying task complexity;
* providing additional examples;
* offering optional support;
* changing interaction patterns;
* allowing different forms of participation;
* providing extension tasks;
* adjusting the amount of scaffolding.

Differentiation should preserve the core learning objective whenever possible.

---

## 13. Adaptation to Learner Profiles

Teachers should pay attention to barriers that may affect participation and learning.

Examples may include:

* different processing speeds;
* unfamiliarity with classroom routines;
* communication anxiety;
* different levels of confidence;
* different linguistic backgrounds;
* accessibility needs;
* different previous learning experiences.

Teachers should focus on practical adaptations that improve access to learning.

When an adaptation requires institutional support or exceeds the teacher's responsibility, the teacher should contact Academic Coordination.

# Part III. Internal Procedures

## 14. Recording Student Feedback

Teachers should record relevant information about student progress according to the school's academic record system.

Records should focus on information that is:

* relevant to learning;
* observable;
* specific;
* useful for future intervention or planning.

Examples include:

* recurring difficulties;
* progress toward learning objectives;
* relevant assessment results;
* participation patterns;
* agreed learning goals;
* interventions already attempted.

Teachers should avoid unnecessary personal information.

---

## 15. Communication with Academic Coordination

Teachers should contact Academic Coordination when a situation requires institutional guidance or a decision beyond normal classroom practice.

Examples include:

* persistent academic difficulties;
* questions about student progression;
* significant attendance concerns;
* requests for extraordinary assessment;
* recurring classroom problems;
* situations involving multiple institutional policies;
* circumstances requiring an exception to a standard procedure.

When contacting the coordination team, teachers should provide:

1. a concise description of the situation;
2. relevant dates or events;
3. actions already taken;
4. available academic evidence;
5. the specific question or support needed.

---

## 16. Recurring Student Difficulties

When a student experiences a recurring difficulty, teachers should follow a basic intervention cycle:

```text
Observe
   ↓
Record
   ↓
Discuss with the student
   ↓
Define an intervention
   ↓
Monitor progress
   ↓
Review the result
```

Teachers should avoid assuming that a single unsuccessful activity represents a stable learning difficulty.

When difficulties persist despite reasonable classroom interventions, Academic Coordination may be consulted.

---

## 17. Escalation Procedures

Not every problem requires escalation.

Teachers should first address ordinary classroom issues within their normal responsibilities.

Escalation is appropriate when:

* the problem persists;
* the situation affects academic progression;
* an institutional policy must be interpreted;
* an exception may be required;
* multiple staff members need to coordinate;
* the teacher does not have authority to make the necessary decision.

Teachers should document relevant information before escalation.

---

## 18. Academic Concerns

Academic concerns may involve:

* persistent difficulty with a skill;
* significant discrepancy between skills;
* insufficient progress;
* repeated failure to meet learning objectives;
* attendance affecting learning;
* difficulties participating in required activities.

Teachers should use evidence from classroom observation, assessments, student work, and feedback records when discussing academic concerns.

Academic concerns should not be based solely on subjective impressions.

---

## 19. Requesting Additional Assessment

A teacher may recommend additional assessment when available evidence suggests that the student's current level or progression requires further investigation.

Examples include:

* significant differences between language skills;
* uncertainty about the student's current level;
* unexpected assessment results;
* evidence that additional information is needed before a progression decision.

Requests should include:

* the reason for the request;
* relevant evidence;
* previous assessments;
* observations;
* the specific question that the additional assessment should address.

The final decision follows the procedures established by the Academic Policy and Student Progression Policy.

---

## 20. Incident Documentation

When an incident requires formal documentation, teachers should record only information relevant to the situation.

Documentation should include, when applicable:

* date and time;
* people involved;
* factual description of what occurred;
* actions taken;
* relevant communications;
* follow-up required.

Teachers should distinguish observable facts from interpretations or assumptions.

Sensitive information should only be recorded and shared through the appropriate institutional channels.

# Part IV. Quick Reference for New Teachers

## Before the Lesson

* Define the learning objective.
* Select activities that support the objective.
* Decide how students will interact.
* Prepare necessary materials.
* Consider possible adaptations.

## During the Lesson

* Monitor student participation.
* Encourage meaningful communication.
* Adjust activities when necessary.
* Provide purposeful feedback.
* Avoid unnecessary correction during fluency work.
* Observe evidence of student learning.

## After the Lesson

* Record relevant progress or concerns.
* Identify students who may need additional support.
* Review whether the learning objective was achieved.
* Plan follow-up when necessary.

## Contact Academic Coordination When

* a student shows persistent academic difficulty;
* progression is unclear;
* an exception to a policy may be necessary;
* additional assessment may be appropriate;
* an issue exceeds normal classroom responsibilities;
* institutional guidance is required.

---

# 21. Core Principles at a Glance

Teachers at Common Ground should remember five central principles:

1. **Communication comes before mechanical performance.**
2. **Students are active participants in their learning.**
3. **English exists in diverse forms and contexts.**
4. **Feedback should lead to action and development.**
5. **Teachers should use evidence and collaborate when academic decisions exceed classroom responsibilities.**

The goal of teaching at Common Ground is not simply to produce students who can reproduce correct sentences. It is to help learners use English effectively, confidently, flexibly, and meaningfully in the contexts that matter to them.
